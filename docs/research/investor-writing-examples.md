# Investor examples for clearer company reports

Reviewed 4 November 2026. Six primary-source documents were opened and read in their relevant sections. The recommendations below are editorial judgments drawn from those examples, not evidence that copying an investor's prose improves forecasts. No valuation, acquisition or analyst run was performed.

## Recommendation

Strengthen the analyst prompt and review a few short examples. Require each important forecast to connect the observed evidence, business mechanism, selected magnitude and shareholder consequence. Finish with a short conclusion that states what the price requires and what could invalidate the case. This develops the existing [output discipline](../analysis-policy.md#output-and-calculation-discipline) and [reader proposal](../planning/return-and-reader-proposal.md#human-report); it does not require a new valuation method or a training project.

The reusable problem is prose that names an attractive business or a risk without explaining its size and financial effect. The benefit is that a reader can assess the analyst's judgment without reconstructing the whole appendix. Keep forward operating EV/EBIT as the primary method under the [valuation policy](../analysis-policy.md#valuation-methods).

## Suggested prompt additions

These are proposed company-neutral instructions. The source examples motivate them; the project's accounting and calculation rules remain authoritative.

1. **Write the conclusion for the reader.** In roughly three to five sentences, state the business case, its dependence on the main operating assumptions, the price/return verdict under the selected hurdle, and the most important reason that verdict could change. Keep any material evidence gap beside that verdict. Munger's owner perspective and Druckenmiller's explicit limits are useful examples. [Munger, item 6(v)](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2014ltr.pdf#page=38), [Druckenmiller, pp. 31–32](https://www.econclubny.org/documents/10184/109144/2019DruckenmillerTranscript.pdf#page=31).
2. **Explain the selected number.** Beside each material forecast, give its compatible historical anchor, the operating change needed, why that change supports the chosen amount, and the strongest contrary evidence. Explain whether growth and margin gains can occur together. A direction such as improving demand does not explain a particular forecast. Pershing Square's operating chains provide a useful presentation example; the requirement for magnitude and contrary evidence comes from [Betaforge's policy](../analysis-policy.md#valuation-methods). [Pershing Square, slides 41 and 45](https://assets.pershingsquareholdings.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/31170156/2024-Annual-Investor-Presentation-1.pdf).
3. **Translate risk into cash and operations.** Name the customer, factory, inventory, receivable, spending or financing mechanism. Show the relevant amount and its effect on cash available for distributions, debt or per-share value. Use the established cash bridge; CFO already includes working-capital movements. Buffett's two letters show why reinvestment belongs beside profit. [1986 appendix](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html), [2007, pp. 7–8](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf#page=6), [project cash discipline](../analysis-policy.md#financial-quality).
4. **Replace vague judgments with a reason.** Phrases such as “credible opportunity” or “insufficient compensation for cash absorption and execution risk” need a concrete explanation in the same paragraph: what must happen, how much cash is tied up, and why the modeled return does or does not cover that uncertainty. Distinguish sales growth, profit growth and per-share growth. This is a proposed editorial application of the project's [output discipline](../analysis-policy.md#output-and-calculation-discipline), not a quotation from any investor.

Review three short passages before expanding the instructions: one forecast rationale, one cash explanation and one conclusion. A useful review asks whether another reader can explain why the selected number is plausible, identify its weakest assumption and state the investment implication. This is a proposed prompt/style-guide check; no improvement in output quality has yet been demonstrated.

## Illustrative rewrite

This fictional manufacturer and every figure below are invented solely to demonstrate writing. They are not an issuer analysis or externally sourced financial evidence. Amounts are in millions of a generic currency; periods and scope are assumed comparable.

| Illustrative case input | Given or assumed |
|---|---|
| Latest annual revenue / EBIT margin / EBIT | 1,000 / 8% / 80 |
| Five-year EBIT margin range | 6%–9% |
| Central-case revenue / margin / EBIT | 1,100 / 9% / 99 |
| Evidence for the 100 revenue increase | Signed orders cover 70; the remaining 30 is an analyst assumption |
| Margin explanation | Higher factory utilization is assumed; no sustained full-year evidence is supplied |
| Forecast CFO / capex | 75 / 50; CFO already includes cash taxes and working-capital movements |
| Forecast cash interest / lease principal outside CFO | 5 / 5 |
| Forecast common dividend | 20 |

Weak wording: “The company offers a credible opportunity, although cash absorption and execution risk limit attractiveness.”

Clearer forecast rationale: “Orders support most of the planned sales increase from 1,000 to 1,100. The remaining 30 still needs customers. EBIT of 99 also requires the margin to rise from 8% to 9%, the top of the historical range. Higher factory utilization could help, but the evidence supplied does not yet establish a full year at that margin. The central case therefore needs both additional sales and a return to peak profitability.”

Clearer cash explanation: “The case produces 15 of cash before common dividends: 75 of operating cash less 50 of capex, 5 of cash interest and 5 of lease principal. A dividend of 20 leaves a funding gap of 5. If borrowing covers that gap, the equity bridge must include the additional debt. Working capital has already reduced operating cash and is not deducted again.”

Short reader conclusion: “Orders support higher sales, but the profit forecast still depends on reaching the best historical margin. The proposed dividend also exceeds the case's cash generation by 5. Treat the case as conditional until the margin evidence and funding gap are reflected in the valuation. These inputs alone do not establish whether the current share price offers the required return.”

The rewrite applies the earnings/capital explanations in [Buffett's 1986 appendix](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html) and [2007 letter, pp. 7–8](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2007ltr.pdf#page=6), alongside the project's [operating assumption](../analysis-policy.md#valuation-methods) and [cash-bridge rules](../analysis-policy.md#financial-quality). The fictional example intentionally supplies no price, multiple, share base or complete equity bridge, so a buy/wait verdict would require additional evidence.
